## Executive framing

**Unconventional Warfare (UW)** is a SOF skillset focused on enabling resistance or insurgency movements to **coerce, disrupt, or overthrow** a government or occupying power, normally by working through or with indigenous resistance structures such as an underground, auxiliary, and guerrilla force in a denied or hostile area. ([Public Intelligence |](https://info.publicintelligence.net/USASOC-UW-PocketGuide.pdf))  

**Resilience and Capacity Building** is the more preventive, partner-facing side of the same logic: helping a state, military, institution, or society prepare to **absorb shock, resist coercion, continue functioning, and recover** from crisis, hybrid warfare, invasion, occupation, terrorism, cyber attack, disinformation, sabotage, or internal instability. NATO defines resilience as the capacity to “prepare for, resist, respond to and quickly recover” from shocks and disruptions. ([NATO](https://www.nato.int/en/what-we-do/deterrence-and-defence/resilience-civil-preparedness-and-article-3))  

The simplest way to understand the relationship is:

> **Resilience is what prevents collapse. Capacity building is how you strengthen the system. Resistance is what remains available if deterrence fails. UW is the SOF method for enabling resistance in support of strategic objectives.**

## 1. Unconventional Warfare: what it is

The US doctrinal definition is important because it is the baseline used by most Western SOF communities:

> UW is conducted to enable a resistance movement or insurgency to coerce, disrupt, or overthrow a government or occupying power by operating through or with an underground, auxiliary, and guerrilla force in a denied area. ([Public Intelligence |](https://info.publicintelligence.net/USASOC-UW-PocketGuide.pdf))

In plain English, UW is not just “guerrilla warfare.” It is a **politico-military campaign** where SOF enable indigenous actors to create strategic effects that conventional forces, diplomacy, or overt military intervention may not be able to achieve alone. It sits inside **Irregular Warfare**, which the US Irregular Warfare Annex describes as a struggle among state and non-state actors to influence populations and legitimacy, using indirect and asymmetric approaches to erode an adversary’s power, influence, and will. ([U.S. Department of War](https://media.defense.gov/2020/Oct/02/2002510472/-1/-1/0/Irregular-Warfare-Annex-to-the-National-Defense-Strategy-Summary.PDF))  

UW therefore has three defining characteristics:

| Characteristic | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Indirect** | SOF usually work through indigenous actors rather than acting as the main force. |
| **Political** | The decisive centre is legitimacy, governance, population support, and strategic narrative. |
| **Long-duration** | UW is rarely a quick tactical action; it requires preparation, trust, networks, sustainment, and political control. |

The most important meeting point: **UW is about creating strategic pressure through resistance, not simply training guerrillas.**

## 2. Core UW components

Western doctrine commonly describes resistance as having several components:

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Underground** | Clandestine network operating in denied or controlled areas. |
| **Auxiliary** | Support network that provides logistics, information, movement support, concealment, medical aid, communications, etc. |
| **Guerrilla force** | Armed irregular element operating in hostile or denied territory. |
| **Public component** | Open political, social, civil, or information activity where possible. |
| **Shadow government / government-in-exile** | Political legitimacy structure that may guide resistance and preserve continuity of authority. |

The USASOC UW guide highlights these as part of a broader resistance ecosystem, not as isolated tactical groups. ([Public Intelligence |](https://info.publicintelligence.net/USASOC-UW-PocketGuide.pdf))  

The doctrinal terms matter because they show that UW is not only a kinetic activity. It depends on **population support, legitimacy, intelligence, logistics, governance, communications, and political direction**.

## 3. Modern UW: what has changed

Traditional UW assumed resistance would often occur in a clearly denied physical area, with clandestine networks growing into guerrilla forces. Modern UW is more complex. David Kilcullen argues that assumptions behind classical UW have diverged from modern reality due to social media, electronic surveillance, diaspora networks, digital connectivity, and the blurred line between overt and covert resistance activity. ([Scandinavian Journal of Military Studies](https://sjms.nu/articles/10.31374/sjms.35))  

Modern resistance may now include:

| Modern factor | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Digital connectivity** | Resistance networks can mobilise, communicate, raise funds, and influence globally. |
| **Diaspora networks** | Support may come from outside the battlespace. |
| **Commercial technology** | Drones, encrypted comms, mapping tools, OSINT, 3D printing, and commercial satellite imagery can matter. |
| **Information warfare** | Narrative, legitimacy, morale, and external support are decisive. |
| **Hybrid threats** | Aggression may occur below the threshold of declared war. |
| **Civil resistance** | Non-violent resistance, civil disobedience, intelligence reporting, and societal non-cooperation can be as strategically important as armed action. |

The implication for SOF: modern UW requires more than classic military skills. It needs **cultural understanding, interagency coordination, information operations literacy, cyber awareness, civil affairs, legal-political judgement, and the ability to work with irregular, civilian, and institutional partners.**

## 4. Resilience: what it means in a SOF context

NATO’s resilience model is a strong reference point. It frames resilience around **whole-of-society preparedness**, not just military readiness. NATO identifies three core civil preparedness functions: **continuity of government, continuity of essential services, and civil support to military operations**. ([NATO](https://www.nato.int/en/what-we-do/deterrence-and-defence/resilience-civil-preparedness-and-article-3))  

NATO’s seven baseline requirements are:

1. Continuity of government and critical government services.  
2. Resilient energy supplies.  
3. Ability to manage uncontrolled movement of people.  
4. Resilient food and water resources.  
5. Ability to manage mass casualties and health crises.  
6. Resilient civil communications systems.  
7. Resilient transport systems. ([NATO](https://www.nato.int/en/what-we-do/deterrence-and-defence/resilience-civil-preparedness-and-article-3))  

For SOF, resilience is not “civil defence” in isolation. It is the foundation that allows a partner nation to:

| Resilience outcome | SOF relevance |
|---|---|
| **Survive shock** | Prevent state paralysis under attack, coercion, or crisis. |
| **Maintain legitimacy** | Help government remain credible and functional. |
| **Support military operations** | Enable host-nation support, mobilisation, logistics, and movement. |
| **Resist hybrid coercion** | Reduce vulnerability to disinformation, proxy networks, cyber attack, sabotage, and intimidation. |
| **Enable resistance if occupied** | Pre-crisis planning makes post-crisis resistance more organised, legitimate, and survivable. |

A strong line for the meeting:

> **Resilience is a deterrent. A state that can absorb disruption, maintain governance, mobilise society, and sustain military operations is harder to coerce, destabilise, or occupy.**

---

## 5. Resistance and the Resistance Operating Concept

The **Resistance Operating Concept (ROC)** is highly relevant. JSOU describes the ROC as focused on developing a **nationally authorised, organised resistance capability prior to invasion or occupation**, as part of layered national defence. It begins with national resilience in the pre-crisis environment and then develops resistance requirements if sovereignty is compromised. ([jsou.edu](https://jsou.edu/Press/Publications?pubdate=1%2F1%2F0001&sort=0&type=full))  

Key idea: resistance should not be an improvised panic response after invasion. It should be:

| Requirement | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Nationally authorised** | Linked to legitimate government authority. |
| **Pre-planned** | Prepared before crisis. |
| **Whole-of-society** | Not just military; involves civil society, infrastructure, private sector, local governance, media, and population. |
| **Legitimate** | Must maintain legal, moral, and political legitimacy. |
| **Layered** | Supports conventional defence, territorial defence, civil preparedness, and continuity of government. |

The ROC also stresses the criticality of maintaining legitimacy during resistance operations and restoring sovereignty. ([jsou.edu](https://jsou.edu/Press/Publications?pubdate=1%2F1%2F0001&sort=0&type=full))  

For the meeting, the conceptual bridge is:

> **Resilience is pre-crisis preparation. Resistance is crisis/occupation continuation. UW is the SOF method that may enable or support resistance.**

---

## 6. Capacity building: the SOF “engine room”

Capacity building is often misunderstood as training courses. In SOF terms, it is much broader. It includes developing **people, institutions, doctrine, leadership, logistics, governance, communications, legal authorities, sustainment, and assessment systems**.

Foreign Internal Defense (FID) is one related concept. Joint doctrine describes FID as civilian and military support to a host nation’s efforts to build capability and capacity to protect society from subversion, lawlessness, and insurgency. ([Fort Benning](https://www.benning.army.mil/infantry/doctrinesupplement/atp3-21.8/PDFs/jp3_22.pdf))  

Security Force Assistance (SFA) is another related concept. US joint doctrine says SFA aims to develop foreign security forces that are **competent, capable, sustainable, committed, and confident**, and it uses development tasks such as **organise, train, equip, rebuild/build, and advise**. ([edocs.nps.edu](https://edocs.nps.edu/2012/December/jdn1_13.pdf))  

The important distinction:

| Concept | Main purpose |
|---|---|
| **UW** | Enable resistance against an adversary, government, or occupying power. |
| **FID** | Support a friendly host nation against internal threats. |
| **SFA** | Build partner security force capability and capacity. |
| **Resilience building** | Strengthen national/societal ability to absorb shocks and continue functioning. |
| **Resistance capacity building** | Prepare a legitimate national resistance capability before crisis or occupation. |

SOF’s comparative advantage is that it can build trust and capability over time through persistent access, language/culture skills, small teams, partner relationships, and politically aware engagement. USSOCOM’s 2025 SOF Renaissance paper frames SOF as operating with allies and partners in more than 80 countries to build partner capacity, execute irregular warfare concepts, and counter adversary strategies below the threshold of armed conflict. ([U.S. Special Operations Command](https://www.socom.mil/Documents/2025-SOF_Renaissance%2825FEB%29Web.pdf))  

---

## 7. Why this matters now

Modern strategic competition is increasingly fought in the grey zone: cyber attacks, sabotage, economic pressure, lawfare, proxy actors, disinformation, political coercion, infrastructure disruption, and internal destabilisation. The US Irregular Warfare Annex explicitly argues that state adversaries and proxies operate below the threshold likely to provoke a conventional response, using disinformation, deception, sabotage, economic coercion, proxies, guerrillas, and covert operations. ([U.S. Department of War](https://media.defense.gov/2020/Oct/02/2002510472/-1/-1/0/Irregular-Warfare-Annex-to-the-National-Defense-Strategy-Summary.PDF))  

That means SOF are valuable not only in war, but also in **competition before war**. Their role is to help partners become difficult to coerce and difficult to defeat.

A useful phrase:

> **The aim is to make the partner force, government, and society a poor target: hard to intimidate, hard to penetrate, hard to paralyse, and hard to occupy.**

## 8. Practical framework for understanding the skillset

Think of UW, resilience, and capacity building across four phases:

| Phase | Focus | SOF contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-crisis** | Resilience, deterrence, partner capacity, legal frameworks, continuity plans, exercises | Assess vulnerabilities, build trusted relationships, advise on readiness, train selected capabilities, support planning. |
| **Crisis / hybrid attack** | Maintain governance, counter coercion, protect critical functions, sustain narrative | Support interagency coordination, crisis planning, information resilience, liaison, partner force readiness. |
| **Conflict / occupation** | Resistance, survival, continuity, intelligence, external support | Enable legitimate resistance structures at strategic/operational level within lawful authorities. |
| **Transition / recovery** | Demobilisation, reintegration, restoration of authority, stabilisation | Support transition planning, partner legitimacy, institutional rebuilding, lessons learned. |

This prevents a narrow interpretation of UW as “behind-the-lines guerrilla warfare.” The real skillset is **strategic preparation of human networks, institutions, and resistance potential**.

## 9. Key capabilities to understand

For meeting purposes, the major capability areas are:

| Capability | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Human domain expertise** | UW and resilience depend on people, identity, grievances, legitimacy, trust, and networks. |
| **Political warfare literacy** | SOF must understand power, governance, legitimacy, influence, and statecraft. |
| **Partner assessment** | You need to know what the partner can actually sustain, not what a training plan assumes. |
| **Institutional development** | Tactical skills fail if logistics, leadership, pay, law, doctrine, and governance are broken. |
| **Civil-military integration** | Resilience requires government, military, private sector, civil society, and infrastructure owners. |
| **Information resilience** | Disinformation, morale, narrative, and public trust are decisive. |
| **Continuity planning** | Government, communications, transport, health, food, water, energy, and command systems must continue under stress. |
| **Exercise design** | Resilience must be tested through realistic exercises, not assumed. |
| **Legal and ethical controls** | Legitimacy and authorisation are essential; uncontrolled armed groups create strategic risk. |

## 10. Key risks and pitfalls

This is where you can sound credible in the meeting.

| Risk | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Legitimacy failure** | Resistance without legitimacy can become criminality, factionalism, or insurgency. |
| **Over-militarisation** | Resilience is whole-of-society; it cannot be owned only by SOF or the military. |
| **Imported models** | Partner systems may not mirror US/NATO structures. SFA doctrine warns against simply importing a US model into a foreign force. ([edocs.nps.edu](https://edocs.nps.edu/2012/December/jdn1_13.pdf)) |
| **Unsustainable training** | Courses that are not linked to doctrine, sustainment, leadership, logistics, and assessment rarely produce durable capability. |
| **“Do no harm” failure** | SFA doctrine warns that donor assistance can unintentionally increase tensions or create adverse effects if the system is poorly understood. ([edocs.nps.edu](https://edocs.nps.edu/2012/December/jdn1_13.pdf)) |
| **Poor authorities** | Resistance preparation is politically sensitive and requires clear legal authority, oversight, and interagency alignment. |
| **Bad measures of success** | Counting courses, students, or equipment is not the same as measuring resilience, readiness, or resistance capacity. |
| **Dependency** | The partner must own the capability; external support should enable, not replace, sovereign capacity. |

## 11. Questions to ask in the meeting

Use these to guide discussion:

1. **What threat are we building resilience against?** Hybrid coercion, internal instability, invasion, occupation, cyber attack, infrastructure disruption, proxy warfare, terrorism, or all of the above?  
2. **Whose capacity are we building?** SOF, conventional forces, police/gendarmerie, civil defence, ministries, local authorities, private infrastructure owners, or national leadership?  
3. **What is the desired end state?** Better partner force tactical capability, national resilience, resistance preparedness, crisis response, or deterrence?  
4. **Who owns the policy authority?** Defence, interior, intelligence, national security council, emergency management, or head-of-government level?  
5. **What must be kept legitimate and lawful?** This is central for resistance and UW-related planning.  
6. **How will we measure success?** Exercises passed, continuity of government tested, comms redundancy proven, partner units independently operating, crisis coordination functioning, public trust maintained.  
7. **What can be done now without becoming politically sensitive?** Resilience assessments, civil-military exercises, continuity planning, critical infrastructure mapping, medical surge, comms redundancy, logistics resilience, partner force professionalisation.

Use this as your main speaking framework:

Resilience and capacity building are the left-of-crisis activities that make a partner nation harder to destabilise, coerce, defeat, or occupy. The two are connected: resilient societies deter aggression, and if deterrence fails, pre-planned legitimate resistance gives the state a way to continue the fight. SOF are valuable because they understand the human domain, can build trusted partner networks, and can operate across the military, civil, political, and information spaces.**

## 13. Suggested one-minute answer if asked “what are these skillsets?”

**Unconventional Warfare** is about enabling legitimate resistance or insurgency forces to create strategic effects against a hostile government or occupying power. It is indirect, political, population-centric, and normally long-duration. It requires deep understanding of legitimacy, human networks, influence, intelligence, sustainment, and partner dynamics.

**Resilience and Capacity Building** is about strengthening a partner’s ability to prepare for, absorb, resist, respond to, and recover from shocks. In a SOF context, it means building the people, institutions, plans, relationships, and capabilities that allow a state to survive hybrid attack, crisis, invasion, or occupation. It is the foundation that makes resistance credible and deterrence stronger.

---

## 14. The line to remember

> **UW is not just fighting unconventionally. It is the deliberate use of resistance, legitimacy, and partner networks to create strategic effect. Resilience and capacity building are how you prepare the ground before the crisis begins.**
